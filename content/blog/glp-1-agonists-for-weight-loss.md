## What Are GLP-1 Agonists for Weight Loss, and How Do They Work?

GLP-1 agonists are prescription medicines that mimic glucagon-like peptide-1, a hormone your gut releases after you eat. They affect appetite, digestion, and blood sugar regulation. For some people, these effects can make it easier to eat less and support weight loss. The medicines don’t work the same way for everyone, though, and they can cause side effects. Whether treatment makes sense depends on your health, goals, and a clinician’s assessment.

### How GLP-1 affects hunger, fullness, blood sugar, and digestion

After a meal, natural GLP-1 helps regulate blood sugar and sends signals related to fullness. GLP-1 medicines act on some of these same pathways. They can prompt the pancreas to release insulin when blood sugar is high, reduce glucagon (a hormone that raises blood sugar), and slow how quickly food moves out of the stomach. They can also affect appetite signals, which may help you feel full sooner or stay full longer. The [Southwoods Health overview of GLP-1 medicines](https://www.southwoodshealth.com/blog/debunking-glp-1-myths) explains these effects. How strongly they occur can vary by medicine and person.

### How appetite changes can support weight loss

When hunger decreases or fullness lasts longer, some people find it easier to eat smaller portions or feel less drawn to food. Over time, eating fewer calories may contribute to weight loss. But appetite changes don’t guarantee a particular result, and people can respond differently to treatment. GLP-1 medicines are generally used as part of a broader weight-management plan, rather than as a replacement for nutrition, physical activity, or other support. [Duly Health and Care discusses how GLP-1 treatment can fit with lifestyle changes](https://www.dulyhealthandcare.com/health-topic/glp-1-medications-for-weight-loss-myths-vs-facts). A clinician can help you consider which changes are realistic and appropriate for your circumstances.

### Why medication works best with individualized care

GLP-1 medicines aren’t suitable for everyone, so treatment should start with an individual medical evaluation. A healthcare professional can review your health history, current medicines, weight-management goals, and potential risks before discussing options. ## Which GLP-1 Medicines Are Approved for Weight Loss?

Several medicines are FDA-approved for chronic weight management in the United States. They have different active ingredients, dosing schedules, and safety considerations. Approval does not mean a medicine is right for everyone, so a healthcare professional should review your health history and treatment goals before recommending one.

### Semaglutide (Wegovy) and liraglutide (Saxenda)

Wegovy contains semaglutide, and Saxenda contains liraglutide. Both act on GLP-1 receptors and are approved for chronic weight management, alongside a reduced-calorie eating plan and increased physical activity, for people who meet the criteria in each medicine’s prescribing information. Those criteria generally include adults with obesity, or adults with overweight and at least one weight-related health condition. The labels also include criteria for certain pediatric patients.

Wegovy is injected once a week; Saxenda is injected daily. Their approved uses, doses, and safety information differ, so one should not be substituted for the other without guidance from a prescriber. Review the [Wegovy prescribing information](https://www.novo-pi.com/wegovy.pdf) and [Saxenda prescribing information](https://www.novo-pi.com/saxenda.pdf) for details about each medicine.

### Tirzepatide (Zepbound): a dual GIP/GLP-1 medicine

Zepbound contains tirzepatide, which acts on both GIP and GLP-1 receptors. It is approved for chronic weight management in eligible adults, alongside a reduced-calorie eating plan and increased physical activity. It is also taken as a weekly injection.

Tirzepatide is not a GLP-1-only medicine. Its dual action, dosing instructions, possible side effects, and precautions differ from those of semaglutide and liraglutide. A clinician can assess whether it may be suitable based on your health history and treatment needs. The [Zepbound prescribing information](https://pi.lilly.com/us/zepbound-uspi.pdf) describes its approved use, dosing, and safety considerations.

### How weight-loss approvals differ from diabetes-labeled medicines

A medicine’s active ingredient and its FDA-approved use are not the same thing. Ozempic and Rybelsus contain semaglutide and are approved for specific uses related to type 2 diabetes and other health risks. Wegovy also contains semaglutide, but it is the brand approved for chronic weight management. Mounjaro contains tirzepatide and is approved for type 2 diabetes, while Zepbound is the tirzepatide brand approved for chronic weight management.

Products with the same active ingredient may have different doses, formulations, approved uses, and insurance coverage. Don’t assume that one brand is automatically a substitute for another, or use someone else’s prescription. Check the [FDA’s drug information](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cder/daf/) and ask a healthcare professional which option, if any, fits your circumstances.

### How dosing schedules and gradual dose increases vary

These medicines generally start at a lower dose, followed by gradual increases according to the product’s prescribing information. Wegovy and Zepbound are weekly injections with stepwise dose increases. Saxenda is taken daily, with its own schedule for increasing the dose. The timing and target dose vary by medicine.

A gradual increase gives the prescriber a chance to assess how you tolerate treatment and may help manage side effects, especially digestive symptoms. Your schedule may depend on your response and the medicine’s instructions. Follow the plan provided for your prescription, rather than copying dosing advice from another person or an online post. If side effects make treatment difficult, contact your prescriber before changing your dose or stopping. The [official Wegovy label](https://www.novo-pi.com/wegovy.pdf) is one example of a product-specific dosing schedule.

### What to know about compounded medicines and prescription safeguards

Compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide are not FDA-approved products. The FDA does not review compounded medicines for safety, effectiveness, or quality before they are marketed. Compounding may be appropriate when a patient’s medical needs cannot be met by an FDA-approved drug, but it is not simply another name for a generic version. The [FDA’s guidance on compounded drugs](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/compounding-and-fda-questions-and-answers) explains how these products differ.

Before starting treatment, ask who will prescribe the medicine, which pharmacy will prepare or dispense it, and how dosing instructions and follow-up care will work. The FDA has reported dosing errors involving compounded semaglutide, including errors when patients measure doses from multi-dose vials. ## How Much Weight Loss Can GLP-1 Medicines Support?

### What trials found for semaglutide, liraglutide, and tirzepatide

Clinical trials have reported average weight loss ranging from about 8% to 21%, depending on the medicine, dose, and study. In a 68-week trial, adults taking semaglutide (Wegovy) lost about 15% of their starting weight on average. With liraglutide (Saxenda), average loss was about 8% over 56 weeks. In a 72-week trial, average weight loss with tirzepatide (Zepbound) ranged from about 15% to 21% across the doses studied. The semaglutide, liraglutide, and tirzepatide trials provide details about their study groups and results.

These figures are averages, not promises or personal forecasts. Participants received lifestyle support as part of the trials, and individual results can differ. Trial outcomes may not reflect what someone will experience in routine care.

### How trial duration and dose shape average results

Weight loss takes time, so a result measured after 40 weeks is not directly comparable with one measured after 68 or 72 weeks. Dose matters, too. These medicines are generally started at a lower dose and increased gradually, based on the medication’s schedule and how a person tolerates treatment. Participants may spend different amounts of time at each dose before a trial measures the final result.

Study design can affect the numbers as well. Trials may differ in participant health, follow-up, and lifestyle support. When comparing results, look at the medicine and dose, the study length, and who took part. [Duly Health and Care explains factors that shape GLP-1 trial results](https://www.dulyhealthandcare.com/health-topic/glp-1-medications-for-weight-loss-myths-vs-facts).

### Why results vary by health history, treatment tolerance, and other factors

A person’s outcome can depend on starting weight, health conditions, other medicines, treatment goals, and response to dose increases. Side effects may affect whether someone can continue treatment or reach a particular dose. A clinician can assess these factors and discuss whether to adjust the plan or consider another option.

The number on the scale is only one part of the picture. Weight loss can include lean muscle as well as fat, so clinicians may also discuss nutrition and physical activity during treatment. The [University of Chicago’s review of GLP-1 medicines](https://anesthesia.uchicago.edu/dr-yuan-senior-author-bmj-review-and-meta-analysis-glp1-agonists) highlights why benefits and potential concerns should be considered in light of each person’s health and treatment goals.

### How results compare with other treatments

A head-to-head trial offers a more direct comparison than separate studies, though its findings still do not determine which medicine is right for every person. In a 72-week trial involving adults with obesity, tirzepatide led to greater average weight loss than semaglutide: about 20% compared with about 14%. The SURMOUNT-5 trial compared the medicines at their maximum tolerated doses in that study population.

Other comparisons can be harder to interpret because studies may use different doses, follow participants for different lengths of time, or include people with different health conditions. For someone with type 2 diabetes, treatment decisions may also need to account for blood sugar management and how other medicines affect weight. ## What Side Effects and Risks Should Patients Know?

### Common digestive effects and strategies clinicians may recommend

Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation, abdominal pain, bloating, gas, and indigestion are common side effects of GLP-1 medicines. Symptoms may improve as the body adjusts, but they can persist or become more noticeable after a dose increase. Follow the prescribed schedule, and contact your care team before changing a dose or stopping treatment.

To help manage digestive discomfort, clinicians may recommend drinking enough fluids, eating smaller meals more often, and limiting fatty, fried, or spicy foods. Skipping meals can make it harder to maintain good nutrition. The [Baylor Scott & White guide to GLP-1 medicines](https://www.bswhealth.com/blog/glp-1-medications-explained-benefits-risks-how-they-work) shares more strategies for managing common effects. Let your clinician know if symptoms continue, worsen, or interfere with daily activities.

### Which serious symptoms and complications need prompt medical attention

Seek prompt medical advice for severe or persistent abdominal pain, especially if it spreads to your back or occurs with ongoing vomiting. It may signal pancreatitis, a rare but serious concern. Severe vomiting or diarrhea can also lead to dehydration and affect kidney function. Contact a healthcare professional if you cannot keep fluids down, urinate much less than usual, or feel faint or unusually weak.

GLP-1 medicines also have product-specific warnings. Some carry a boxed warning about thyroid C-cell tumors observed in animal studies. Other serious concerns may include gallbladder problems, kidney injury related to dehydration, and worsening diabetic retinopathy in some people. The Cleveland Clinic’s guide to GLP-1 agonists explains potential complications and symptoms. For severe symptoms, seek urgent care instead of waiting for a routine appointment.

### Which warnings and precautions may make treatment unsuitable

Certain GLP-1 medicines should not be used by people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma, or by people with multiple endocrine neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN 2). These medicines are generally not used for weight loss during pregnancy. If you are pregnant, planning a pregnancy, or breastfeeding, discuss your options and medication timing with a healthcare professional.

Other factors, including a history of pancreatitis, gallbladder or kidney problems, digestive conditions, and your overall health, may affect whether treatment is appropriate. ### How diabetes medicines and other prescriptions affect safety

GLP-1 medicines typically have a low risk of causing low blood sugar on their own. That risk may increase when they are taken with insulin or certain diabetes medicines, such as sulfonylureas. A clinician may adjust the dose of those medicines and explain how to recognize and respond to low blood sugar. Don’t change or stop any medication without medical guidance.

Tell your clinician about all prescription medicines, over-the-counter drugs, and supplements you take. GLP-1 medicines slow stomach emptying, which may affect how some oral medicines are absorbed. Whether this matters depends on the specific drugs and your health needs, so ask your clinician or pharmacist about potential interactions. ### What may happen to appetite and weight after treatment stops

After stopping a GLP-1 medicine, some people notice that appetite or cravings return, and some regain weight. Blood sugar may also rise for people taking treatment to help manage it. The amount and timing of these changes vary, and no one can predict exactly how you’ll respond. Some medication-related effects, such as nausea or feeling unusually full, may ease after treatment ends.

Talk with your prescriber before stopping, even if you’ve reached a weight goal or are having side effects.
