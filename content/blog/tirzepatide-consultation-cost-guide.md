## What Is Tirzepatide, and Why Do You Need an Evaluation?

Tirzepatide is a prescription medicine that acts on receptors for two hormones, GIP and GLP-1. It is the active ingredient in Mounjaro, which is approved to help improve blood sugar control in adults with type 2 diabetes, and Zepbound, which is approved for chronic weight management in certain adults. The [FDA-approved Zepbound prescribing information](https://pi.lilly.com/us/zepbound-uspi.pdf) describes its uses, dosing, warnings, and possible side effects.

Before prescribing tirzepatide, a clinician needs to assess whether it may be appropriate for you. That evaluation can include your health history, current medicines, treatment goals, and potential risks. It also gives you a chance to ask about expected benefits, side effects, alternatives, follow-up, and costs. An evaluation does not guarantee a prescription. FitClinicMD says its planned [weight-management care](https://www.fitclinicmd.com/treatments/weight-management) will involve provider-led decisions, but the clinic is not yet open to patients.

### How tirzepatide affects weight and blood sugar

Tirzepatide activates GIP and GLP-1 receptors, which are involved in blood sugar regulation and appetite. Depending on the product and a person’s health needs, treatment may help improve blood sugar control or support weight management. For weight management, medication is intended to be used alongside a reduced-calorie eating plan and increased physical activity. Tirzepatide also slows stomach emptying, especially early in treatment, and may cause digestive side effects such as nausea, diarrhea, or constipation.

It is not right for everyone, and results vary. A clinician can explain which uses are approved, what benefits may be realistic for you, and what risks to consider. The [Zepbound prescribing information](https://pi.lilly.com/us/zepbound-uspi.pdf) includes important safety details, including warnings and situations in which the medication should not be used.

### What clinicians review: your health history, medications, goals, and risks

A clinician may ask about your weight history, previous treatment attempts, health conditions, and what you hope to achieve. They will usually review prescription medicines, over-the-counter products, and supplements, since these can affect treatment choices. Be prepared to discuss digestive issues, blood sugar concerns, allergies, and relevant family history. Tell the clinician if you are pregnant, planning to become pregnant, or breastfeeding, since this may affect care recommendations.

The provider will weigh potential benefits against possible risks and consider whether another option may better fit your needs. For example, the [FDA prescribing information](https://pi.lilly.com/us/zepbound-uspi.pdf) lists specific contraindications and warnings that can influence prescribing decisions. Sharing complete, accurate information helps your clinician make a recommendation based on more than weight alone.

### Why an evaluation may not lead to a prescription

A consultation is an assessment, not a promise of medication. A clinician may decide tirzepatide is not appropriate because of your health history, current medicines, potential risks, or the medication’s approved uses. They may also need more information before making a recommendation, or suggest a different treatment plan that better matches your needs and goals.

If the clinician does not recommend a prescription, ask them to explain why and what options you can consider instead. Depending on your situation, next steps could include further assessment, another treatment, or support with nutrition and physical activity. A provider should explain the decision clearly and give you room to ask questions. FitClinicMD describes an [education-first approach to peptide therapy](https://www.fitclinicmd.com/treatments/peptides), with treatment decisions made by a licensed provider rather than assumed in advance.

### When clinicians may recommend labs and follow-up

Lab work is not automatically needed for every patient. A clinician may recommend tests based on your medical history, symptoms, medicines, or the information needed to assess whether treatment is suitable. Ask what each test is intended to check, where you can complete it, and whether the cost is included in the consultation or billed separately.

Follow-up visits let a clinician review your response, discuss side effects, and decide whether changes to your care are appropriate. The schedule depends on your circumstances and treatment plan. Before starting, ask how follow-ups are arranged, what they cost, and how to contact the care team with concerns between visits. Before scheduling, ask the clinic for a written estimate that separates the consultation fee from other possible charges. Request costs for the initial evaluation, follow-up visits, lab work, medication, shipping, and any program or membership fees. Prices and services vary, so confirm fees directly with the clinic. Keep the estimate and ask for an explanation of any charge that is not listed.

### Confirm what the consultation includes and what costs extra

“Consultation” can mean different things from one clinic to another. Ask whether the fee covers a clinician’s review of your health history, medications, treatment goals, and potential risks. Find out whether the appointment is by phone or video, how long it lasts, and whether the clinician will discuss next steps and answer follow-up questions.

Then clarify what is billed separately. Lab work, follow-up visits, medication, pharmacy dispensing, and delivery may cost extra. Some telehealth programs bundle consultation and medication costs, while others charge for them separately, as [InCare notes in its overview of online tirzepatide treatment](https://www.incarenow.com/tirzepatide-online-prescription-what-tampa-patients-should-know). ### Ask about insurance, payment options, cancellations, and refunds

Ask whether insurance may cover the consultation, lab work, or medication. Coverage varies by plan and service, so check with your insurer rather than assuming a particular cost will be reimbursed. If prior authorization may be needed, ask who submits the request and whether the clinic charges for that help.

For self-pay, confirm accepted payment methods, when charges are due, and whether installment options are available. Read the cancellation and refund policies before paying. Ask what happens if you cancel late, miss an appointment, or a clinician determines that treatment is not appropriate. Check for recurring charges and how to stop them. Request clear answers in writing.

### Compare total expected costs, not consultation fees alone

A low consultation fee does not always mean a lower overall cost. Compare the expected expense over several months, including the evaluation, medication, lab work, follow-up visits, membership fees, and delivery. Ask whether the medication price applies to every dose and whether it could change as your care plan changes. If the clinic cannot provide a reliable total, ask which charges are fixed and which depend on your treatment.

Use the same checklist for each clinic. One program may include follow-up care in its monthly price, while another may bill for every visit. Costs can also depend on dose, clinic structure, and which services are included, as [InCare explains in its budgeting guide](https://www.incarenow.com/how-to-budget-for-tirzepatide-costs-in-2026). Treat an unusually low advertised price as a reason to ask what is excluded, not as a guarantee of what you will pay.

### Verify clinician licensing and ask how the provider selects a pharmacy

Before paying or sharing health information, confirm that the clinician evaluating you is licensed to practice in your state. Ask for the clinician’s name and credentials, then check the license through your state medical board. A responsible evaluation should include a review of your health history and medications. The clinician may also recommend lab work when it is clinically appropriate. An evaluation does not guarantee a prescription.

If medication is prescribed, ask which pharmacy will fill it and how the clinic handles questions about dispensing and delivery. Ask whether the prescription would be for an FDA-approved product or a compounded medication. Compounded drugs are not FDA-approved, and the [FDA explains how compounded drugs differ from FDA-approved medicines](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/compounding-and-fda-questions-and-answers).
