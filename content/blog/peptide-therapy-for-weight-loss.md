## What Is Peptide Therapy for Weight Loss?

Peptides are short chains of amino acids. Some act as signals in the body, and certain prescription medicines for weight management work by targeting hormone pathways involved in appetite, digestion, and blood sugar. Semaglutide acts on GLP-1 receptors, while tirzepatide acts on both GLP-1 and GIP receptors.

“Peptide therapy” can refer to very different products and approaches. A prescription medicine with evidence for weight management is not the same as a wellness product or a peptide sold for research use. The name alone does not tell you whether a product is safe, effective, or appropriate for you. Its ingredients, intended use, evidence, and medical oversight all matter.

### How GLP-1 and GIP medicines affect appetite, fullness, and blood sugar

GLP-1 is a hormone involved in blood sugar regulation and digestion. Medicines that act on GLP-1 receptors can help the body release insulin when blood sugar is high. They can also slow stomach emptying and affect appetite, so some people feel full sooner or stay full longer. These effects may help a person eat less, though results vary. Healthline explains how [GLP-1 medicines can affect appetite and fullness](https://www.healthline.com/health/weight-loss/using-peptides-for-weight).

Tirzepatide also acts on GIP receptors, another pathway involved in blood sugar regulation. The effects of these medicines are not identical for everyone, and a medication may not suit every person. A licensed clinician can review your medical history, current medicines, and goals before discussing whether a GLP-1 or GIP-based treatment is appropriate.

### How prescription medicines differ from wellness and research peptides

Prescription medicines for weight management have specific ingredients, dosing instructions, safety information, and FDA-reviewed uses. They require a prescription and should be taken with guidance from a qualified clinician. Wellness products marketed for weight loss and products labeled for research use are different. They may not be FDA-approved for human use, and their claims may not be supported by reliable clinical evidence.

The word “peptide” does not establish a product’s quality, safety, or effectiveness. ## Which Prescription Peptides Have Evidence for Weight Loss?

Some prescription medicines used for weight management are peptide-based drugs. The best-studied options include semaglutide, tirzepatide, and liraglutide. Semaglutide and liraglutide act on GLP-1 receptors, while tirzepatide acts on both GIP and GLP-1 receptors. These hormone pathways help regulate appetite and blood sugar, but the medicines have different approved uses, dosing schedules, and risks.

Clinical trials show that these medicines can help many people lose weight when used with reduced-calorie eating and increased physical activity. The results below are averages from specific studies, not predictions for an individual. Participants received treatment and lifestyle support under trial conditions, which may differ from someone’s experience in routine care.

### Semaglutide (Wegovy): STEP 1 results and dosing

Semaglutide is a once-weekly injection approved for chronic weight management in eligible patients. In the 68-week STEP 1 trial, adults with overweight or obesity who did not have diabetes lost an average of 14.9% of their body weight with semaglutide, compared with 2.4% with placebo. Both groups also received lifestyle support. The STEP 1 study describes the trial design and results.

Treatment typically starts at a low dose and increases gradually, which can help limit digestive side effects. The maintenance dose for weight management is 2.4 mg once weekly, if prescribed and tolerated. Wegovy is also approved for certain adolescents age 12 and older with obesity. Eligibility, dose, and formulation depend on the patient and current prescribing information, so a clinician should confirm what applies.

### Tirzepatide (Zepbound): SURMOUNT-1 results across doses

Tirzepatide is a once-weekly injection that acts on both GIP and GLP-1 hormone receptors. In the 72-week SURMOUNT-1 trial, average weight change was −15.0% at the 5 mg dose, −19.5% at 10 mg, and −20.9% at 15 mg. Participants who received placebo lost an average of 3.1%. The study enrolled adults with obesity or overweight and at least one weight-related health condition, but not diabetes. The SURMOUNT-1 trial explains its methods and findings.

Zepbound doses are increased gradually. A prescriber selects a maintenance dose based on the patient’s response and ability to tolerate treatment. The trial averages do not mean that a higher dose is the right choice for everyone. A clinician should consider medical history, side effects, and treatment goals before adjusting a dose or continuing therapy.

### Liraglutide (Saxenda): an approved option with a different dosing schedule

Liraglutide is a GLP-1 medicine approved for chronic weight management. Unlike Wegovy and Zepbound, it is taken as a daily injection. In the 56-week SCALE trial, participants taking liraglutide lost an average of 8.0% of their body weight, compared with 2.6% among participants taking placebo. The study also included lifestyle counseling. The SCALE trial provides details about participants and results.

Saxenda is gradually increased to a 3 mg daily maintenance dose, if prescribed and tolerated. Some people may prefer a daily schedule, while others may find weekly dosing easier to manage. Liraglutide has its own eligibility criteria and safety information. Before treatment, ask a clinician to review the medicine’s [FDA prescribing information](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2023/206321s016lbl.pdf) and discuss whether it fits your needs.

### Compare trial results without treating separate studies as head-to-head evidence

The percentages from STEP 1, SURMOUNT-1, and SCALE show how each medicine performed in its own study. They are not a direct comparison. The trials ran for different lengths of time, enrolled different participant groups, and used different protocols. Differences in health history, lifestyle support, and study methods can also affect results.

For example, the average weight change in SURMOUNT-1 cannot show how tirzepatide would compare with semaglutide for the same person. That question requires a trial designed to compare the medicines directly. When discussing options with a clinician, consider approved uses, possible side effects, dosing schedules, health needs, and follow-up plans. Trial percentages can provide context, but they cannot predict an individual result or determine which treatment is best for you.

### Who may qualify, why results vary, and why treatment may be long term

For adults, prescription weight-management medicines are commonly considered for people with a BMI of 30 or higher, or a BMI of 27 or higher with a weight-related condition, such as high blood pressure or type 2 diabetes. Criteria vary by medicine. Wegovy also has an indication for certain adolescents age 12 and older with obesity, so adult criteria should not be applied to younger patients. A clinician can review eligibility against the medicine’s [FDA-approved labeling](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2024/215256s011lbl.pdf).

Weight loss is not guaranteed. Results may depend on dose, health history, treatment consistency, eating and activity patterns, and individual response. These medicines may be part of long-term weight care, but treatment duration should be decided with a clinician. Weight regain can happen after stopping, so discuss follow-up, maintenance options, and alternatives before starting.

## How Do Approved Treatments Differ From Experimental Peptides?

A peptide’s popularity online does not show that it is safe or effective for weight loss. Consider what the medicine is approved to treat, what human evidence supports its use, and whether a licensed clinician will assess and monitor your care.

### FDA-approved weight-management medicines and their labeled uses

Wegovy (semaglutide) and Zepbound (tirzepatide) are FDA-approved for chronic weight management in eligible patients. They are used alongside reduced-calorie eating and increased physical activity. Whether either medicine is appropriate depends on factors such as your health history, other medications, and treatment goals.

Brand names and approved uses matter. Ozempic also contains semaglutide, and Mounjaro contains tirzepatide, but those brands are approved for type 2 diabetes, not weight management. Approval for one condition does not mean every brand containing the same ingredient has the same labeled use. The FDA’s [Wegovy prescribing information](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2024/215256s011lbl.pdf) describes its approved use, dosing, and safety information. A clinician can help you understand which options, if any, fit your circumstances.

### Why AOD-9604, BPC-157, and CJC-1295 are not established weight-loss treatments

AOD-9604, BPC-157, and CJC-1295 are sometimes promoted for fat loss, metabolism, recovery, or muscle support. Those claims do not establish that the substances work or are safe for weight loss. Evidence in people is limited, and some claims rely largely on animal or cell studies. AOD-9604, in particular, has not shown consistent weight-loss results in the evidence reviewed by [Rite Aid’s peptide overview](https://riteaid.com/peptides/weight-loss).

These substances are not FDA-approved weight-management medicines. Be cautious if a seller or clinic presents them as proven alternatives to approved treatments or guarantees results. Ask what human studies support the proposed use, what is known about potential risks, and whether a licensed clinician will assess your health and provide follow-up care.

### Compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide: prescriptions, oversight, and quality limits

Compounded medicines are prepared by pharmacies under specific conditions, such as when an approved drug cannot meet a patient’s medical needs. They are not FDA-approved versions of brand-name medicines, and the FDA does not review each compounded product for safety, effectiveness, and quality before it is marketed. A prescription and pharmacy oversight matter, but they do not make a compounded medicine equivalent to an FDA-approved product.

The FDA has reported dosing errors involving compounded injectable semaglutide, including errors related to vial concentrations and measuring doses. It has also warned that semaglutide sodium and semaglutide acetate are different salt forms from the active ingredient in FDA-approved medicines. Review the agency’s safety information on compounded semaglutide, and ask a clinician or pharmacist about the product, dose, instructions, and source.

### Risks of research-use-only products and unclear sourcing

Products labeled “research use only” or “not for human consumption” are not intended to be used as medicines. Their ingredients, concentration, handling, and quality may be difficult to verify. An online description, customer review, or certificate of analysis is not proof that a product is safe to inject or effective for weight loss.

Using an unverified product may expose you to incorrect dosing, contamination, mislabeled ingredients, or unexpected reactions. The FDA has warned consumers about unapproved GLP-1 products sold through online vendors and other sources. If you are considering medication-based care, seek an evaluation from a licensed clinician and ask what product would be prescribed, who will dispense it, and how your treatment will be monitored.

## What Are the Risks, and How Can You Reduce Them?

Prescription medicines used for weight management can help some people, but they also carry risks. Side effects and precautions depend on the medication, dose, health history, and other medicines you take. A clinician should review these factors before treatment and continue to monitor your health if you start a medication.

Before an evaluation, make a list of your health conditions, medicines, supplements, allergies, and any past reactions to treatment. Share changes in your health promptly, and ask what symptoms need urgent attention. Read the medication guide for the specific prescription you receive, since warnings and instructions can differ between medicines.

In any setting, treatment should be based on an individual assessment rather than an automatic prescription. ### Common digestive side effects and dose increases

Nausea, diarrhea, constipation, vomiting, and abdominal discomfort are common side effects of GLP-1 medicines. They may be more noticeable when you first start treatment or when your dose increases. A clinician may recommend gradual dose increases to help manage side effects, but the schedule depends on the medicine and your response.

Don’t increase, repeat, or change a dose on your own. Contact your clinician if symptoms persist, worsen, or make it difficult to eat or drink. The [Wegovy prescribing information](https://www.novo-pi.com/wegovy.pdf) and [Zepbound prescribing information](https://pi.lilly.com/us/zepbound-uspi.pdf) explain dosing and possible side effects for each medicine. Follow the instructions for your own prescription rather than assuming different medications have the same schedule.

### Serious risks: gallbladder problems, pancreatitis, dehydration, and low blood sugar

GLP-1 medicines can be associated with gallbladder problems and pancreatitis. Seek prompt medical care for severe, ongoing abdominal pain, particularly if it spreads to your back or occurs with vomiting. Persistent vomiting or diarrhea can also lead to dehydration and may affect kidney function. Contact a healthcare professional if you can’t keep fluids down.

Low blood sugar is more likely when these medicines are taken with insulin or certain diabetes medicines, including sulfonylureas. Your clinician may need to review your medications and blood sugar plan. Fainting, confusion, or severe symptoms require urgent attention. The [prescribing information for semaglutide](https://www.novo-pi.com/wegovy.pdf) and [tirzepatide](https://pi.lilly.com/us/zepbound-uspi.pdf) describes these risks and relevant warning signs.

### Thyroid tumor warning, contraindications, pregnancy, and other health factors

Semaglutide and tirzepatide carry a boxed warning about thyroid C-cell tumors observed in rodents. It is not known whether these medicines cause this type of tumor in humans. They should not be used by people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma, or by people with multiple endocrine neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN 2). Tell your clinician about your medical history before considering treatment.

Weight-management medicines are not for use during pregnancy. If you are pregnant, planning a pregnancy, or could become pregnant, discuss your options with a clinician before starting treatment. The recommended timing for stopping a medicine before pregnancy varies, so check its prescribing information. A history of pancreatitis, gallbladder disease, or other health conditions may also affect whether treatment is appropriate.

### Discuss other medicines and planned procedures with a clinician

Share a complete list of your prescription medicines, over-the-counter drugs, vitamins, and supplements before starting treatment. Some medicines affect blood sugar or digestion, and taking multiple products with a GLP-1 or related ingredient may increase safety concerns. Don’t combine medications or peptides unless a qualified clinician familiar with your health history has reviewed the plan.

Tell your care team if you have a procedure or surgery planned, especially one involving anesthesia or deep sedation. These medicines can slow stomach emptying, which may affect procedure planning. Let both the clinician managing your medication and the procedure team know what you take and when you last took it. The [Zepbound prescribing information](https://pi.lilly.com/us/zepbound-uspi.pdf) includes information about delayed stomach emptying and procedures requiring anesthesia.

### Follow prescribed doses, attend follow-ups, and know when to seek care

Take your medicine only as prescribed. Don’t take an extra dose to make up for a missed one unless the instructions for your specific medicine tell you to. Keep follow-up appointments so your clinician can review side effects, progress, other medications, and whether the treatment plan still suits your needs. Ask your care team before changing or stopping treatment.

Seek urgent medical help for severe or persistent abdominal pain, signs of an allergic reaction, fainting, or symptoms of dangerously low blood sugar. Contact a healthcare professional promptly if you can’t keep fluids down or have signs of dehydration.

If a medication is compounded, ask the prescriber and pharmacy about its source, instructions, and dosing. Compounded medicines are not FDA-approved, and the FDA has reported concerns including dosing errors with compounded GLP-1 drugs. Review the FDA’s safety information on compounded GLP-1 medicines, and never use a product labeled for research purposes.

## How Can You Choose Reputable, Clinician-Guided Care?

Choosing care for weight loss means looking beyond a medication name or a low introductory price. A reputable program should explain what is known about treatment, assess whether it may be appropriate for you, and make it easy to ask questions before you commit. A prescription should follow a clinical evaluation, not serve as the starting point.

Check that the care team can explain its credentials, how follow-up works, where medication comes from, and what the full cost may include. Ask for evidence behind any claims, especially when a service promotes peptides that are not approved for weight management. You should be given time to weigh options and understand possible risks.

No online program can promise a specific result or determine that a treatment is right for everyone. Your health history, goals, other medications, and response to treatment all matter. Use the questions below to assess whether a provider offers thoughtful medical care, clear communication, and realistic expectations.

### What to expect from an evaluation, personalized plan, and ongoing monitoring

A responsible program starts with a health evaluation, not a promise of a prescription. A clinician should ask about your medical history, current medications, weight-management goals, and relevant health conditions. They may recommend lab work when clinically appropriate, then explain whether an approved medicine or another approach could fit your needs.

If treatment is appropriate, ask how the plan will be tailored and what follow-up includes. Monitoring helps a clinician review your response and side effects, then decide whether to adjust or stop treatment. Weight management may also involve nutrition, physical activity, and behavioral support. Medication is one part of care, not a substitute for an individualized plan. Before enrolling, confirm how often follow-ups happen and whom to contact if a concern comes up between appointments.

### Check clinician licenses, pharmacy sources, and patient support

Confirm that the clinician evaluating you is licensed to practice in your state. You can check a physician’s credentials through your state medical board or the Federation of State Medical Boards’ physician search. Ask who will provide follow-up care and how to reach the clinical team if you have questions or experience side effects.

If medication is prescribed, ask which pharmacy will dispense it and how to contact that pharmacy. For a compounded medication, ask why it is being considered and what quality safeguards apply. Compounded drugs are not FDA-approved, and the FDA does not review them for safety, effectiveness, or quality before they are marketed. The agency explains the [limits and risks of compounded drugs](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/compounding-and-fda-questions-and-answers).

### Verify claims with FDA resources, prescribing information, and clinical-trial records

Look for evidence behind a treatment claim. For approved medicines, the FDA-approved prescribing information describes labeled uses, dosing, warnings, and known side effects. The [FDA’s drug database](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cder/daf/) can help you confirm whether a medicine is approved and find its official labeling.

For claims about weight loss, check whether they rely on published clinical trials in people, rather than testimonials, animal research, or early-stage findings. [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/) lists registered studies, their status, and available results. A study result does not guarantee an individual outcome. Results from separate trials also should not be treated as a direct comparison unless the treatments were tested head to head.

### Ask about treatment options, risks, costs, and realistic outcomes

Before starting, ask which options may suit your health history, why a clinician recommends a particular treatment, and what benefits and risks are known. Find out how dosing works, which side effects to watch for, and when you should contact the care team. Ask what the next step would be if the medication does not help or causes problems.

Clarify the full cost before agreeing to care. Request a breakdown of the evaluation, medication, lab work, follow-up visits, and recurring fees. Insurance coverage varies, and some services or medications may require out-of-pocket payment. A reputable provider should explain costs and coverage plainly, avoid promises about reimbursement, and give you time to consider your choices.

### Red flags: guaranteed results, automatic prescribing, and pressure to buy

Be cautious of services that guarantee a specific amount of weight loss, promise results on a fixed timeline, or claim a treatment works for everyone. Outcomes vary, and a clinician cannot responsibly predict exactly how one person will respond. A brief online form should not replace a medical evaluation.

Automatic prescribing is another warning sign. You should be able to discuss whether treatment is suitable, review alternatives, and ask questions before making a decision. Take care with providers that pressure you to pay immediately, promote products as risk-free, or make dramatic claims without pointing to reliable evidence. If you feel rushed or uncertain, pause before enrolling and consider asking another qualified clinician for an opinion.
