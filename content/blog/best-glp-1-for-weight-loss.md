### How GLP-1s Affect Appetite, Fullness, Digestion, and Blood Sugar

GLP-1 receptor agonists act like glucagon-like peptide-1, a hormone released in the gut. They can affect appetite and fullness, help the pancreas release insulin when blood sugar is elevated, and slow how quickly food leaves the stomach. These effects may support weight management, but individual responses vary.

## Which Medications Have FDA Approval for Weight Management?

Several prescription medications have FDA-approved uses for chronic weight management. This section focuses on the three compared here: semaglutide (Wegovy), tirzepatide (Zepbound), and liraglutide (Saxenda). Each is used alongside reduced-calorie eating and increased physical activity. Approval doesn’t mean a medication is right for everyone. A clinician considers the specific product’s labeling, your health history, and potential risks before recommending treatment.

### Semaglutide (Wegovy), Tirzepatide (Zepbound), and Liraglutide (Saxenda)

Wegovy, Zepbound, and Saxenda are the FDA-approved brand names for these medications when prescribed for weight management. Semaglutide and liraglutide act on GLP-1 receptors. Tirzepatide acts on both GIP and GLP-1 receptors. Wegovy and Zepbound are injected weekly; Saxenda is injected daily, so their schedules and dosing plans differ.

The FDA approves Wegovy for eligible adults and adolescents ages 12 and older with obesity. Saxenda is approved for eligible adults and certain adolescents ages 12 and older who meet product-specific weight and obesity criteria. Zepbound is approved for chronic weight management in adults. Review the [FDA prescribing information for Wegovy](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2024/215256s011lbl.pdf), [Saxenda](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2023/206321s016lbl.pdf), and Zepbound for each product’s approved uses and directions.

### Weight-Management Brands vs. Medications for Other Uses

A shared active ingredient doesn’t make two brands interchangeable. Wegovy contains semaglutide and has an FDA-approved chronic weight-management use. Ozempic and Rybelsus also contain semaglutide, but their FDA-approved uses are for type 2 diabetes. Zepbound contains tirzepatide and is approved for chronic weight management in adults; Mounjaro contains tirzepatide and is approved for type 2 diabetes.

These distinctions matter because products can have different approved uses, formulations, and dosing instructions. A medication approved for diabetes isn’t automatically an FDA-approved weight-management option just because it contains the same active ingredient as a weight-management brand. Ask your prescriber which product’s labeling applies to your care, and don’t switch brands or adjust doses without medical guidance.

### FDA-Approved Uses, Eligibility, and Age Considerations

For adults, the weight-management labels for these medications generally include people with a BMI of 30 or higher, or a BMI of 27 or higher with at least one weight-related condition. The exact criteria depend on the medication. BMI is one part of an assessment, not a complete picture of your health or treatment needs.

Age eligibility differs by product. Wegovy and Saxenda have approved uses for certain adolescents with obesity, but the requirements are product-specific. For example, Saxenda’s adolescent indication includes criteria related to age, body weight, and obesity. Zepbound’s weight-management indication is for adults. A clinician can review your medical history, medications, and goals alongside the [FDA-approved product information](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cder/daf/) to determine whether treatment may be appropriate.

### Average Trial Results for Tirzepatide, Semaglutide, and Liraglutide

In a 72-week trial of adults with obesity who did not have type 2 diabetes, participants taking tirzepatide lost an average of 20.2% of their starting weight. Participants taking semaglutide lost an average of 13.7%. Both groups received lifestyle counseling, and the results reflect the trial’s doses and conditions. ## What Side Effects and Safety Risks Should You Know About?

GLP-1 medications can support weight management, but they can also cause side effects and carry important risks. The details vary by medication and health history, so review the medication guide with your prescriber and share all medications and supplements you take.

### Common Digestive Side Effects and How Clinicians Can Help

Nausea, diarrhea, vomiting, constipation, abdominal discomfort, indigestion, and reduced appetite are common side effects. They may be more noticeable when you start treatment or increase your dose. Some people find symptoms ease over time; others need help managing them.

Your clinician can review your symptoms, hydration, and eating habits, and decide whether to adjust the pace of dose increases or consider another treatment. Don’t change your dose without medical guidance. Ongoing vomiting or diarrhea can cause dehydration and may affect kidney function. The [Wegovy prescribing information](https://www.novo-pi.com/wegovy.pdf) explains common side effects and advises patients to contact a healthcare professional about persistent or severe symptoms.

### Serious Risks, Warnings, Contraindications, and Interactions

Less common but serious risks can include pancreatitis, gallbladder problems, severe digestive reactions, kidney injury related to dehydration, and serious allergic reactions. Low blood sugar is a greater concern when certain GLP-1 medications are taken with insulin or some other diabetes medicines. Your prescriber can review your full medication list and determine whether changes or closer monitoring are needed.

Semaglutide, tirzepatide, and liraglutide carry a boxed warning about thyroid C-cell tumors observed in rodents. These medications generally should not be used by people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or multiple endocrine neoplasia syndrome type 2. Tell your prescriber about your health and family history, and review the medication-specific warnings in the [Zepbound prescribing information](https://pi.lilly.com/us/zepbound-uspi.pdf).

### Pregnancy, Planned Procedures, and Symptoms That Need Prompt Care

Weight-loss treatment is not recommended during pregnancy. If you become pregnant while taking a GLP-1 medication, contact your prescriber promptly. Semaglutide should be stopped at least two months before a planned pregnancy, according to its [prescribing information](https://www.novo-pi.com/wegovy.pdf). Guidance can differ by medication, so discuss your plans with your clinician before trying to conceive.

Tell your healthcare team before surgery or a procedure involving anesthesia or deep sedation. GLP-1 medications can slow stomach emptying, which may affect procedure planning. The American Society of Anesthesiologists’ guidance recommends that patients discuss their medication and individual risk with their care team rather than stopping it on their own. Seek prompt medical care for severe, persistent abdominal pain, signs of an allergic reaction, or ongoing vomiting or diarrhea.

## What Should You Know About Compounded GLP-1 Medications?

Compounded GLP-1 medications are sometimes discussed as alternatives to FDA-approved drugs, but the two are not interchangeable by default. If a compounded medication is being considered, ask why it may be appropriate for your situation and how its risks, ingredients, and dosing will be managed.

### How Compounded Products Differ From FDA-Approved Medications

FDA-approved medications, including Wegovy (semaglutide) and Zepbound (tirzepatide), are reviewed for safety and effectiveness for specific uses and manufactured under FDA quality standards. Compounded medications are prepared by pharmacies for individual patients, generally when an approved medication cannot meet a patient’s medical need. The FDA does not review or approve compounded drugs for safety, effectiveness, or quality before they are marketed. Read the agency’s explanation of [how compounding differs from FDA approval](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/compounding-and-fda-questions-and-answers).

For compounded semaglutide, ask which form of the ingredient is used. The FDA has cautioned that semaglutide salt forms, such as semaglutide sodium or acetate, differ from the active ingredient in FDA-approved medications, and their safety and effectiveness have not been established. The agency has also received reports of dosing errors involving compounded semaglutide, including errors when measuring doses from multidose vials. Review the FDA’s safety information on compounded semaglutide with your clinician if this option comes up.

### Ask About the Pharmacy, Ingredients, Dosing, and Clinical Oversight

Ask which pharmacy prepares the medication and whether it is a state-licensed 503A pharmacy or an FDA-registered 503B outsourcing facility. FDA registration does not mean a compounded medication is FDA-approved. Find out the exact active ingredient and formulation, how to measure each dose, how to store the medication, and what to do if you miss a dose or experience side effects.

A licensed clinician should review your health history, explain why a compounded medication is being considered, and discuss alternatives, including FDA-approved treatments. Ask how your dose and response will be monitored, when follow-up visits will happen, and whom to contact with concerns. Clear instructions matter, especially if a medication comes in a multidose vial and requires you to measure each dose yourself. Don’t rely on a seller’s claims alone: your prescriber should be able to explain the medication’s source, potential risks, and plan for ongoing care.
