## Which Prescription Weight Loss Medications Are FDA-Approved?

The FDA has approved several prescription medications for long-term weight management, but they are not interchangeable. They differ in how they work, who may be eligible, and whether they come as a pill or an injection. Long-term options for adults include orlistat, phentermine-topiramate, naltrexone-bupropion, liraglutide, semaglutide, and tirzepatide. Phentermine by itself is generally approved for short-term use. Setmelanotide is intended for people with certain rare genetic conditions, not for weight management in the general population.

Approval also depends on the specific medicine and its labeled use. Some options are approved for certain children and adolescents, while others are approved for adults only. A medication’s approval does not mean it is suitable for every person. A clinician should review health history, current medications, and treatment goals before recommending an option. The [NIDDK guide to prescription medications](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/weight-management/prescription-medications-treat-overweight-obesity) summarizes approved uses and age ranges. FitClinicMD also explains its planned approach to [GLP-1 care](https://www.fitclinicmd.com/glp-1), which would begin with an individual clinical assessment.

### Long-term options: orlistat (Xenical), phentermine-topiramate (Qsymia), and naltrexone-bupropion (Contrave)

These medicines are approved for long-term weight management, but they work differently. Orlistat reduces the amount of fat absorbed from food. Phentermine-topiramate combines phentermine, which can reduce appetite, with topiramate, which may support appetite control and fullness. Naltrexone-bupropion affects brain pathways involved in appetite and food cravings.

Their forms and dosing schedules vary. Orlistat is a capsule taken with meals that contain fat. Qsymia is taken as a capsule, while Contrave is taken as tablets on a prescribed schedule. Qsymia is also approved for some adolescents age 12 and older with obesity. Each medicine has its own precautions, possible side effects, and reasons a clinician may advise against its use. A healthcare professional can review these factors and discuss whether one may fit your needs. The [NIDDK medication guide](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/weight-management/prescription-medications-treat-overweight-obesity) provides further details.

### GLP-1 and related options: liraglutide (Saxenda), semaglutide (Wegovy), and tirzepatide (Zepbound)

Saxenda, Wegovy, and Zepbound are injections prescribed for weight management in eligible patients. Liraglutide (Saxenda) is taken daily, while semaglutide (Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Zepbound) are taken weekly. These medications act on hormone pathways involved in appetite and fullness. Tirzepatide works through two pathways, GIP and GLP-1.

Their approved age ranges differ. Saxenda and Wegovy are approved for some children and adolescents age 12 and older, as well as adults. Zepbound is approved for adults. The right option depends on the medication’s current prescribing information and a person’s health profile. Medications with the same active ingredient may have different brand names and approved uses, so they should not be treated as interchangeable. Learn more about FitClinicMD’s planned [weight-management care](https://www.fitclinicmd.com/treatments/weight-management).

### Setmelanotide (Imcivree) for certain rare genetic conditions

Setmelanotide (Imcivree) is approved for people with certain rare genetic conditions that can cause severe obesity. It is not a general weight-loss medication. Eligibility requires a diagnosis of a specific condition, typically confirmed through genetic testing, and the medication is given as a daily injection.

Because treatment is limited to particular genetic conditions, a specialist may need to review test results and medical history before determining whether it is appropriate. The approved age and eligible conditions depend on the medicine’s current prescribing information. The [NIDDK overview of prescription weight-management medicines](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/weight-management/prescription-medications-treat-overweight-obesity) describes setmelanotide and its specific use.

### Short-term options, including phentermine, and why they aren’t for long-term use

Phentermine is an appetite-suppressing medication approved for short-term use, generally for a few weeks. Evidence about its safety and effectiveness over longer periods is limited, so phentermine alone is not approved as a long-term weight-management medication. It may also be unsuitable for people with certain health conditions or medication histories.

Phentermine is one ingredient in Qsymia, which has a separate FDA approval for long-term weight management. The approval of a combination medicine does not mean each ingredient has the same approval when used alone. Before starting phentermine, ask a clinician about the intended treatment period, potential risks, and monitoring. The [NIDDK explains the limits of evidence for longer-term phentermine use](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/weight-management/prescription-medications-treat-overweight-obesity).

### Compare approved uses, age requirements, and pill or injection forms

This overview summarizes the age ranges and forms described by the NIDDK. Approval and eligibility depend on the medication’s specific labeling and a clinician’s assessment.

* **Orlistat (Xenical):** capsule; approved for adults and children age 12 and older.
* **Phentermine-topiramate (Qsymia):** capsule; approved for adults and some children age 12 and older.
* **Naltrexone-bupropion (Contrave):** tablets; approved for adults.
* **Liraglutide (Saxenda):** daily injection; approved for adults and some children age 12 and older.
* **Semaglutide (Wegovy):** weekly injection; approved for adults and some children age 12 and older.
* **Tirzepatide (Zepbound):** weekly injection; approved for adults.
* **Setmelanotide (Imcivree):** daily injection; approved for certain genetically confirmed conditions, with age requirements that depend on the condition.
* **Phentermine alone:** pill or capsule; approved for short-term use, generally in adults.

Age ranges are not a substitute for an individual evaluation. A licensed clinician can check current prescribing information, review health factors, and discuss which options, if any, may be appropriate.

## How Do Prescription Weight Loss Medications Work?

### Learn how medications reduce appetite, increase fullness, affect cravings, or limit fat absorption

Prescription weight loss medications act on different processes involved in appetite, eating, and digestion. GLP-1 medicines, including semaglutide and liraglutide, affect signals that help regulate appetite and fullness. Tirzepatide acts on both GIP and GLP-1 pathways, which are involved in appetite and food intake. Other medications work differently: phentermine-topiramate and naltrexone-bupropion affect brain pathways linked to appetite or cravings, while orlistat reduces the amount of fat absorbed from food.

These medicines can support weight management, but they do not replace nutrition, physical activity, sleep, or other parts of a care plan. The [National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/weight-management/prescription-medications-treat-overweight-obesity) provides an overview of how prescription options work and how they may be used alongside lifestyle changes.

### Compare medication classes and the conditions they treat

Common prescription options for long-term weight management include orlistat, phentermine-topiramate, naltrexone-bupropion, liraglutide, semaglutide, and tirzepatide. Some are taken as pills, while others are injections. Their approved uses, dosing schedules, potential side effects, and precautions differ, so one medication may be a better fit than another based on a person’s health and treatment goals.

Setmelanotide is a specialized medicine for certain rare genetic conditions associated with obesity. It is not a general treatment for weight management. Some weight loss medicines may also have approvals for other conditions, but the right choice depends on the individual and the specific product. A clinician can review the FDA’s information on prescription weight management medicines and consider the benefits and risks for your situation.

### Set realistic expectations for results, timelines, dose increases, and individual differences

Weight change usually takes time, and results vary from person to person. Some medications begin at a lower dose, followed by gradual increases based on the prescribing instructions. This can give the body time to adjust and may help manage side effects. The schedule depends on the medication, and dose changes should be guided by a healthcare professional.

During treatment, a clinician may review weight trends, health measures, side effects, and progress toward your goals. Your response can also be affected by your health, the medication, and other parts of your care plan. There is no single timeline or result that applies to everyone. The [NIDDK’s guidance on prescription weight management medicines](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/weight-management/prescription-medications-treat-overweight-obesity) emphasizes that medication is generally used as part of a broader approach to weight management.

### Understand that results aren’t guaranteed and weight may return after stopping

A prescription medicine cannot guarantee a particular amount of weight loss, and a treatment that works for one person may not be right for another. Side effects, cost, limited progress, or changes in health may lead a clinician and patient to adjust the plan, switch medications, or stop treatment. Weight regain can happen after stopping some medications, so it is helpful to discuss ongoing care and next steps before beginning treatment.

Do not change your dose or stop a prescribed medicine without talking with your clinician. ## Who May Qualify for Prescription Weight Loss Medication?

Prescription weight loss medication may be an option for some people as part of a broader care plan. Eligibility depends on more than weight or BMI: a clinician also considers your health, medical history, other medicines, and the risks and benefits of a specific treatment.

### Meet common criteria: a BMI of 30 or higher, or 27 or higher with a weight-related condition

For adults, prescription weight management medication may be considered at a BMI of 30 or higher, or at a BMI of 27 or higher when a weight-related health condition is also present. Examples include high blood pressure, type 2 diabetes, and sleep apnea, according to the [National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/weight-management/prescription-medications-treat-overweight-obesity).

These are general criteria, not a guarantee that a medication is right for you or that a clinician will prescribe it. BMI is a screening measure, and each medication has its own approved uses and age requirements. A clinician can assess your situation and discuss whether medication could complement nutrition, physical activity, and other care.

### Review how health conditions, medical history, current medicines, and past efforts affect eligibility

A clinician will consider your overall health, not just your BMI. They may ask about current and past health conditions, family history, prescription medicines and supplements, previous weight management efforts, and your goals. These details can affect which options are appropriate and whether a treatment’s potential risks outweigh its benefits. The [Obesity Medicine Association’s guide to weight loss medications](https://obesitymedicine.org/blog/weight-loss-medications/) outlines some of the factors that inform medication choice.

Before an appointment, make a list of your medicines and supplements, and note what you have tried for weight management and how it worked for you. Share any side effects or concerns, too. Depending on your history and the treatment being considered, a clinician may recommend lab tests or other checks before deciding on next steps.

### Consider how pregnancy, plans for pregnancy, and other factors may affect treatment

Pregnancy, plans to become pregnant, and breastfeeding can affect whether weight loss medication is appropriate. The medications discussed in the [Obesity Medicine Association’s medication overview](https://obesitymedicine.org/blog/weight-loss-medications/) are not approved for use during pregnancy, and none are approved for use while breastfeeding. Research on use during breastfeeding is limited.

Tell your clinician if you are pregnant, breastfeeding, trying to conceive, or could become pregnant. They can explain the guidance for a specific medication and discuss suitable options. Other considerations, such as side-effect risks, current medicines, treatment costs, and your preferences, may also shape the decision. Don’t start, stop, or change a medication based on general information alone. Ask a licensed healthcare professional for advice based on your circumstances.

### Get a licensed clinician’s assessment to determine eligibility and medication choice

Meeting common BMI criteria does not mean you automatically qualify for medication. A licensed clinician can review your health and discuss whether medication, a lifestyle program, or another approach fits your needs. The NIDDK notes that some people may lose weight through a program focused on behavior, eating habits, and physical activity. For others, medication may be considered as part of a broader plan.

## What Benefits, Side Effects, and Safety Risks Should You Consider?

### Weigh potential benefits for weight and health against what treatment can’t promise

Prescription weight loss medication can help some people lose weight and maintain that loss, especially when combined with nutrition, physical activity, and ongoing clinical care. Results vary based on the medication, dose, health history, and individual response. The [National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/weight-management/prescription-medications-treat-overweight-obesity) reports that, after one year, adults taking medication as part of a lifestyle program lost an average of 3% to 12% more of their starting weight than those in a lifestyle program without medication. That range reflects results across studies, not a promise for any one person.

Treatment may also improve some weight-related health measures, but it cannot guarantee a specific result or replace regular care. A clinician can help set realistic goals and review your progress. ### Recognize common side effects, including nausea, constipation, diarrhea, and headache

Side effects depend on the medication. With GLP-1 and related medicines, common effects can include nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation, bloating, and stomach pain. Headaches may also occur. Digestive symptoms can be more noticeable when treatment starts or the dose increases. They may improve over time, but persistent or severe symptoms deserve medical attention.

Before starting a medication, ask your prescriber what side effects to expect and how to manage them. Contact your clinician if symptoms interfere with eating, drinking, or daily activities, or if they continue or worsen. Don’t change your dose or stop treatment without medical guidance, unless you’re experiencing an emergency and need immediate care. The [GLP-1 safety overview from Walgreens](https://www.walgreens.com/topic/virtual-healthcare/weight-loss.jsp) describes common digestive effects and other considerations to discuss with a healthcare professional.

### Identify serious risks, contraindications, warning signs, and drug interactions

Risks vary by medication. Some GLP-1 medicines carry an FDA boxed warning about thyroid C-cell tumors observed in animal studies. These medicines should not be used by people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid cancer or by people with multiple endocrine neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN 2). Other reported risks include pancreatitis, gallbladder problems, low blood sugar, kidney injury related to dehydration, severe allergic reactions, changes in heart rate, and delayed stomach emptying.

Seek urgent medical care for severe or persistent abdominal pain, signs of a serious allergic reaction, or severe dehydration. Tell your prescriber about all medicines and supplements you take. Some diabetes medicines can increase the risk of low blood sugar when used with GLP-1 treatment, and delayed stomach emptying may affect some oral medicines. Review the [medication safety information](https://www.walgreens.com/topic/virtual-healthcare/weight-loss.jsp) with your clinician, including whether pregnancy or plans for pregnancy affect your treatment options.

### Correct common misconceptions: medication isn’t a quick fix, doesn’t suit everyone, and GLP-1s aren’t only for people with diabetes

Prescription weight loss medication is one possible part of obesity care, not a quick fix or a substitute for individualized support. It isn’t suitable for everyone. A clinician will consider your medical history, current medicines, health goals, and the risks and benefits of each option before recommending treatment. Some people may need a different approach or may not qualify for a particular medication.

GLP-1 medicines are not only used for diabetes. Some medicines in this group have approved uses for chronic weight management in eligible patients, while others are approved for diabetes or different conditions. The specific drug, dose, and reason for prescribing matter. Read about FitClinicMD’s approach to [GLP-1 treatment](https://www.fitclinicmd.com/glp-1), then discuss with a licensed clinician whether a particular medicine is appropriate for you.

### Discuss long-term use, dependence concerns, and weight regain with a clinician

Weight management medication may be a long-term treatment. A plateau does not automatically mean a medicine has stopped helping. You and your clinician can review whether to continue, adjust the dose, switch medications, or consider another plan. Some people regain weight after stopping treatment, as changes in appetite and other biological factors may persist after weight loss. The [Obesity Medicine Association’s overview](https://obesitymedicine.org/blog/weight-loss-medications/) explains why treatment and monitoring may continue after initial progress.

Dependence risks also differ by medication. GLP-1 medicines are not generally considered addictive, while phentermine is a stimulant with potential misuse and dependence risks and is usually prescribed for short-term use. Never share medication or take more than prescribed. Before starting treatment, ask about expected duration, follow-up visits, and what to do if you want to stop. A clinician can help you weigh those decisions in light of your health and the specific medication.

## How Do Prescription Medications Compare With OTC Products and Lifestyle Changes?

### Get an evaluation and ongoing clinical oversight for prescription medicines

Prescription weight loss medicines require an assessment from a licensed healthcare professional. A clinician can review your medical history, current medicines, weight-related health conditions, and goals to decide whether treatment may be appropriate. If it is, follow-up care can help track your progress, discuss side effects, and determine whether the plan needs to change.

Medication can support weight loss, but results vary. The [National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/weight-management/prescription-medications-treat-overweight-obesity) reports that, after one year, adults taking prescription medication as part of a lifestyle program lose an average of 3% to 12% more of their starting body weight than those in a lifestyle program without medication. Your own results may differ, and a clinician can help you set realistic expectations.

### Distinguish OTC orlistat from supplements, which may have limited evidence and variable quality

Over-the-counter products include both medicines and dietary supplements, and they are not interchangeable. Low-dose orlistat, sold as Alli, reduces the amount of fat your body absorbs. It can cause digestive side effects, especially after meals high in fat. Ask a healthcare professional or pharmacist whether it is suitable for you, particularly if you have a health condition or take other medicines.

Weight loss supplements may contain ingredients with limited evidence, and product quality can vary. Being sold over the counter does not mean a supplement has been proven safe and effective for weight loss. The [National Institutes of Health Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/WeightLoss-HealthProfessional/) summarizes evidence and safety concerns for common ingredients. Check with a clinician before trying a supplement, especially alongside prescription treatment.

### Include nutrition, movement, sleep, and behavioral support in weight management

Medication is one possible part of a weight management plan, not a replacement for practical support and healthy routines. Depending on your needs, a plan may include nutrition guidance, regular physical activity, strength training, sleep support, and help with behaviors or circumstances that affect eating and movement. Choose changes that fit your health, preferences, and daily life.

The [Obesity Medicine Association](https://obesitymedicine.org/blog/weight-loss-medications/) recommends combining medication with healthy eating, regular activity, and behavioral support. These approaches can help you build sustainable habits while treatment addresses factors such as appetite or fullness. A healthcare professional can help you set manageable goals and revisit them as your needs, health, or response to treatment changes.

### Use medication to support long-term care, not replace it

Weight management may require ongoing care. Some people regain weight after stopping medication because appetite and other body signals can change again. That does not mean treatment has failed, or that everyone needs to take medication indefinitely. The right approach depends on the specific medicine, your progress, side effects, health needs, and preferences.

Talk with your clinician before changing or stopping a prescription. Together, you can review your progress, consider what to expect if treatment changes, and plan support for maintaining results. ## How Can You Choose a Medication With a Healthcare Professional?

Choosing a prescription weight loss medication is a shared decision. A clinician can help you compare options based on your health, goals, preferences, and daily routine, then check how treatment is working over time.

### Weigh your health goals, potential benefits and risks, preferences, and practical needs

Start by talking about what you hope treatment will help you achieve. Your goals may include weight loss, improving a weight-related health condition, or finding an approach that is easier to maintain. Ask what benefits are realistic, what side effects may occur, and how long it might take to assess your response.

Practical details matter, too. Consider whether you prefer a pill or injection, how often you’re comfortable taking medication, and whether cost or insurance coverage affects your choices. The [National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/weight-management/prescription-medications-treat-overweight-obesity) recommends discussing potential benefits alongside side effects, other health conditions and medicines, family history, and cost. Bring your questions to the appointment so you can compare options together.

### Review your medical history, other medicines, dosing schedule, and preferred form

Share your health history, including current or past conditions, allergies, previous weight management treatments, and relevant family history. Tell your clinician about all prescription medicines, over-the-counter products, and supplements you take. These details can help identify possible interactions or reasons a particular medication may not suit you.

Ask how each option is taken and whether the dose changes over time. Some medications are pills, while others are injections, and their schedules differ. Your comfort with the form and routine can affect how well a treatment fits your life. The [Obesity Medicine Association’s overview of weight loss medications](https://obesitymedicine.org/blog/weight-loss-medications/) also highlights health conditions, current medicines, family history, side-effect risks, cost, and treatment goals as factors to consider with a clinician.

### Set treatment goals and decide how to assess progress and side effects

Before starting, agree on how you’ll review progress. Weight is one measure, but you may also want to discuss changes in a weight-related health condition, mobility, or other health markers. Ask when you’ll check in, which side effects are common, and which symptoms should prompt you to contact your care team sooner.

Medication response varies from person to person, so discuss what you’ll do if a treatment isn’t effective or causes difficult side effects. The Obesity Medicine Association notes that a clinician may consider a different medication if a patient has not lost at least 5% of body weight after 12 to 16 weeks at the highest tolerated dose. Ask whether that benchmark applies to your situation and how your clinician will assess your individual progress.

### Plan follow-ups, dose changes, switching medications, or stopping safely

Ask how often you’ll have follow-up appointments and whether lab tests may be appropriate. Regular check-ins give you and your clinician time to review progress, side effects, and changes in your health. Together, you can decide whether to adjust the dose, consider another medication, or revisit your goals.

Don’t change your dose or stop a prescription without discussing it with the clinician managing your care. The right approach depends on the medication and your circumstances. If you’re considering telehealth, confirm that the clinician is licensed to treat patients in your state and ask how follow-up care works. ### Compare compounded medications with FDA-approved drugs

Compounded medications are prepared by a pharmacy for an individual patient. They are not FDA-approved, and the FDA does not review them for safety, effectiveness, or quality before marketing in the same way it reviews approved drugs. Compounding may be appropriate in specific circumstances, but it should not be treated as an interchangeable, lower-cost version of an FDA-approved medication.

If a compounded product is being considered, ask why it is appropriate for your situation, which pharmacy will prepare it, and how the dose and use instructions will be explained. Compounding rules depend on the medication and applicable FDA requirements. Be cautious of claims that a compounded product is identical to an approved drug. ## What Should Long-Term, Physician-Guided Weight Management Include?

Long-term weight management is not just a decision about medication. It starts with an understanding of a person’s health, goals, and circumstances, then continues with regular reviews of progress and any changes in health or treatment needs. A clinician may consider weight history, medical conditions, current medicines, previous weight-management efforts, and personal preferences when discussing possible options.

Prescription medication may be part of care for some people, but it does not guarantee a particular result or replace other forms of support. Nutrition, physical activity, sleep, and behavioral support may all be relevant to a person’s plan.

Good care also leaves room for questions and shared decisions. A clinician can explain potential benefits, limitations, side effects, and other options, then work with the patient to decide what may be appropriate. Regular follow-up helps ensure that decisions reflect how treatment is actually going, rather than relying on assumptions or a one-time evaluation.

### Get an individualized evaluation, with labs when clinically indicated

An individualized evaluation helps a clinician assess whether prescription treatment may be appropriate and which options could suit a person’s health needs. The discussion may cover weight history, health conditions, current medicines, allergies, past efforts, and treatment goals. Preferences also matter, including how a person feels about a medication’s form, schedule, and potential costs.

Lab tests are not automatically needed for everyone. A clinician may recommend testing when results could clarify a health concern, inform a treatment decision, or help guide monitoring. Ask what a test is intended to assess and how the result could affect your care plan. ### Review progress, side effects, and overall health regularly

Follow-up appointments give the patient and clinician a chance to discuss whether treatment is helping, whether side effects have appeared, and whether the plan still fits the patient’s needs. Progress is not measured only by weight. Changes in health, appetite, daily habits, energy, and how well a person tolerates treatment may also be useful to discuss. Keeping notes about symptoms and questions can make those conversations more specific.

Some side effects may be manageable, while others may require prompt medical attention or a change in treatment. Contact your clinician about concerns, and do not change a dose or stop a prescription without discussing it with the prescriber. The [NIDDK’s information on prescription weight-loss medicines](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/weight-management/prescription-medications-treat-overweight-obesity) notes that results differ among people and medications. Regular reviews help guide decisions based on an individual’s response.

### Make informed decisions through education and shared planning, not automatic prescribing

A prescription should follow a clinical evaluation, not take its place. Before starting treatment, ask what the medication is intended to do, what side effects or risks to watch for, and how you and your clinician will assess progress. You can also discuss other possible approaches, dosing, cost, and how long treatment might continue. If you are considering stopping, ask what to expect and how to do so safely.

Shared planning means your questions and preferences are part of the decision. A clinician can explain available information and help weigh potential benefits and risks, but a particular treatment may not be suitable for everyone. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does FDA approval mean a weight loss medication is right for me?**\
No. Approval applies to a medicine’s specific uses and patient groups. A licensed clinician needs to consider your health history, current medications, and treatment goals before deciding whether it may be appropriate.

**Can I get weight loss medication through FitClinicMD now?**\
Not yet. FitClinicMD is still in pre-launch and is not currently accepting patients. Its planned service includes online evaluations and follow-up care, with prescriptions considered only when a provider determines they are appropriate.

**Are compounded medications the same as FDA-approved medications?**\
No. Compounded medications are not FDA-approved, and the FDA does not review them for safety, effectiveness, or quality before marketing as it does with approved drugs. Ask why a compounded medication is being considered and which pharmacy would prepare it.

**What should I bring up during a weight management evaluation?**\
Share your health conditions, medications and supplements, previous weight management efforts, and treatment goals. It can also help to ask about possible side effects, costs, follow-up appointments, and how progress will be assessed.

**Can I stop taking a prescription weight loss medication whenever I want?**\
Talk with your prescriber before changing your dose or stopping treatment. The right approach depends on the medication and your health, and your clinician can discuss what to expect and plan any changes with you.
