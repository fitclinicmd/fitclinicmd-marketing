## What Is an Online GLP-1 Consultation?

An online GLP-1 consultation is a medical appointment conducted remotely, often by phone or video. Before the visit, you may be asked to complete an intake form with details about your health history, current medications, weight-related goals, and any conditions that could affect treatment. ### What can a virtual visit do—and not do?

A virtual visit lets a licensed clinician review your health history, discuss your goals, and consider whether treatment or further evaluation may be appropriate. A prescription should follow an individual medical assessment, not simply a completed questionnaire or a request for a particular medication. For some GLP-1 medications prescribed for weight management, adults may be considered if they have a body mass index (BMI) of 30 or higher, or a BMI of 27 or higher along with a weight-related health condition. Examples include high blood pressure, type 2 diabetes, and sleep apnea. These are general criteria, not a diagnosis or an assurance that treatment is suitable. ## What Should You Know About GLP-1 Safety and Compounded Medications?

GLP-1 treatment involves more than choosing a medication or dose. A clinician should review your health history, explain potential risks, and discuss how your care will be monitored. Ask where a medication comes from and who to contact if you have questions or side effects. These details are especially important if a compounded medication is being considered.

### Discuss side effects, warnings, and medication interactions

GLP-1 medications can cause side effects such as nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation, or abdominal discomfort. Warnings differ by medication, so share your medical history and a complete list of prescription medicines, over-the-counter products, and supplements with the clinician. Ask which risks apply to the specific medication being discussed and whether it could affect your existing conditions or interact with another treatment.

Some GLP-1 products have warnings about serious risks, including pancreatitis or gallbladder problems. Certain medications also carry a boxed warning about thyroid C-cell tumors and should not be used by people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or multiple endocrine neoplasia syndrome type 2. Review the medication’s [FDA prescribing information](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cder/daf/) with your clinician, since warnings vary by product.

### Ask how clinicians will monitor your health and treatment response

Follow-up is an important part of treatment. Ask how often you will check in, which symptoms to report, and whether lab work may be appropriate based on your health history. A clinician may discuss side effects, hydration, nutrition, physical activity, sleep, weight changes, and other health measures. Monitoring should reflect your needs, rather than relying on automatic dose increases.

Taking more than prescribed, dosing more often, or increasing a dose too quickly can lead to harm. The FDA’s information on compounded semaglutide describes adverse events associated with dosing errors. Follow the instructions provided for your specific medication, and do not change your dose without guidance from your clinician. If the instructions for an injectable medication are unclear, ask the clinician or dispensing pharmacy to explain how to measure and administer it.

### Compare FDA-approved and compounded medications

FDA-approved medications have been reviewed for safety and effectiveness for their approved uses. Compounded drugs are prepared by a pharmacist or physician to meet a specific medical need, but they are not FDA-approved. The FDA does not review compounded drugs for safety, effectiveness, or quality before they are marketed. Ask why a compounded medication is being considered, whether an FDA-approved option may suit your needs, and what risks and limitations apply.

Ask what the compounded product contains and where it will be prepared. The FDA has warned against compounded products made with semaglutide sodium or semaglutide acetate, which are different active ingredients from the semaglutide in FDA-approved medicines. Its [guidance on drug compounding](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/compounding-and-fda-questions-and-answers) explains what patients should know. A clinician should explain the proposed medication clearly, without suggesting that a compounded product is identical to an FDA-approved one.

### Confirm how the pharmacy prepares, checks, and fulfills medications

Before a prescription is sent, ask for the pharmacy’s name, location, and contact information. Confirm that it is licensed to serve patients in your state, and ask how to check its credentials. The FDA’s BeSafeRx guidance offers steps for evaluating online pharmacies.

Ask how the medication will be labeled, what storage instructions apply, and whom to contact with questions. For an injectable product, make sure you understand the concentration, dose, syringe markings, and schedule before using it. FDA has reported dosing errors involving compounded semaglutide, including confusion about how much to draw into a syringe. Don’t guess if a label or instruction is unclear. Contact the prescribing clinician or pharmacy for guidance before taking the medication.

### Know when to seek prompt medical advice

Contact your clinician promptly about persistent or severe abdominal pain, repeated vomiting, signs of dehydration, or symptoms that are unusual or getting worse. Severe abdominal pain, especially if it spreads to your back, needs timely medical assessment. For trouble breathing, swelling of the face or throat, fainting, or another emergency, call 911 or seek emergency care.

Before starting treatment, ask whom to contact after hours and keep the clinic’s and pharmacy’s contact details handy. If you think you took the wrong dose or used the medication incorrectly, contact a healthcare professional or poison control promptly. Don’t take an extra dose to make up for a missed one or change your schedule without medical guidance. Patients and clinicians can also report suspected medication problems through the FDA’s [MedWatch reporting program](https://www.fda.gov/safety/medwatch-fda-safety-information-and-adverse-event-reporting-program).
